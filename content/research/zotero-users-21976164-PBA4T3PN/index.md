# Management of Fibromyalgia Syndrome

## Source facts

Don L Goldenberg, Carol Burckhardt, Leslie Crofford

## Machine interpretation

This 2004 JAMA clinical review by Goldenberg, Burckhardt, and Crofford synthesizes 505 articles to develop evidence-based fibromyalgia management guidance\. The authors judged low-dose tricyclic medications, aerobic exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy, patient education, and multidisciplinary programs to have the strongest evidence available at that time\. Medication benefits were generally modest and predominantly short-term\. The review highlights inadequate masking, small samples, inconsistent outcome measures, and uncertain long-term effectiveness\. Its treatment rankings, diagnostic framework, and regulatory statements reflect evidence available through 2004, not current clinical guidance\.

## Key findings

- The panel reviewed 505 articles and ranked efficacy evidence as strong, moderate, or weak according to study design and consistency of positive findings\.
- Tricyclic agents, particularly amitriptyline and cyclobenzaprine, received the strongest medication evidence rating, but overall benefit was described as modest\.
- The review reports significant clinical responses to tricyclic agents in 25% to 37% of patients; in a 208-patient trial, early improvement was no longer evident at 26 weeks\.
- Aerobic exercise, CBT, patient education, and multidisciplinary programs received strong evidence ratings, although nonmedicinal trials generally lacked blinding\.
- Tramadol, selected SSRIs and SNRIs, and pregabalin had promising but less established evidence; some findings were available only as conference abstracts\.
- Acupuncture results were mixed: one electroacupuncture trial favored treatment over sham, while another reported similar improvement with traditional and sham acupuncture\.
- Many commonly used therapies lacked adequate evaluation\. Missing trials do not establish that a therapy is ineffective\.

## Limitations

- The evidence search ends in 2004; conclusions do not incorporate subsequent trials, diagnostic criteria, or regulatory developments\.
- Most medication trials lasted only 6 to 12 weeks, limiting conclusions about sustained effectiveness in a chronic condition\.
- The authors identify small samples, inadequate blinding, and nonstandardized outcome measures in medication trials\.
- Exercise, education, and CBT trials generally used wait-list, flexibility, or education controls and could not be adequately blinded, leaving possible expectancy and attention effects\.
- Some emerging medication and acupuncture findings were cited as abstracts without complete trial reports\.
- Although databases and grading criteria are described, the supplied text does not provide a reproducible search strategy, study-selection flow, or detailed study-level risk-of-bias assessment\.
- The review reports no treatment trials including patients with concurrent rheumatic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus, limiting generalizability\.
- Few trials combined medication with nonpharmacological interventions in ways that isolated each component's contribution\.
- Evidence classifications are not entirely consistent: massage is grouped with weak evidence in the treatment box but described as having moderate evidence in the conclusions\.
- No total unique participant count is reported across the reviewed literature; individual trial and review sample sizes cannot be safely summed\.

## Why this is relevant

- Provides a historical baseline for comparing later fibromyalgia reviews and treatment guidelines\.
- Useful for research on multimodal chronic pain management and patient-centered outcomes\.
- Highlights methodological challenges in behavioral and exercise trials, especially masking and comparator choice\.
- Identifies gaps in long-term effectiveness, combined-treatment evaluation, and subgroup-specific treatment response\.
