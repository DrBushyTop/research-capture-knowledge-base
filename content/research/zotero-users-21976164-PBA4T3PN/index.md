# Management of Fibromyalgia Syndrome

Don L Goldenberg, Carol Burckhardt, Leslie Crofford

## Machine interpretation

This 2004 JAMA clinical review by Goldenberg, Burckhardt, and Crofford synthesizes fibromyalgia treatment evidence from 505 reviewed articles\. The authors rated evidence strongest for low-dose tricyclic agents, cardiovascular exercise, cognitive behavioral therapy, patient education, and multidisciplinary interventions\. Medication benefits were generally modest and short-term, while some nonpharmacological treatment gains persisted during follow-up\. The review proposes a stepwise management framework, but its conclusions reflect evidence available through 2004 rather than current guidance\.

## Key findings

- The authors classified evidence as strong for amitriptyline and cyclobenzaprine, although overall medication efficacy was modest and most trials lasted only 6–12 weeks\.
- The review reports that antidepressant meta-analyses found improvements in sleep, pain, fatigue, and well-being; tender-point pain did not consistently improve\.
- Cardiovascular exercise was associated with improvements in fitness, pain, and tender-point pressure thresholds, with benefits dependent on continued exercise\.
- Patient education, cognitive behavioral therapy, and multidisciplinary programs improved selected symptom, functional, and self-efficacy outcomes; some gains persisted for months or longer\.
- Evidence for tramadol, fluoxetine, newer serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, and pregabalin was rated moderate, with some findings based on single trials or incompletely published results\.
- Many commonly used treatments lacked adequate evaluation\. Absence of trial evidence for a therapy was not equivalent to demonstrated ineffectiveness\.

## Limitations

- The literature search extends only through 2004; treatment rankings, diagnostic criteria, and regulatory statements are historical\.
- Many medication trials were small, short, inadequately blinded, or used nonstandardized outcome measures, limiting confidence in sustained benefit\.
- Exercise, education, and cognitive behavioral therapy trials were not adequately blinded and often used wait-list, education, or flexibility comparators\.
- The review provides broad database coverage and evidence-ranking rules but does not report a reproducible full search strategy, study-selection flow, or detailed study-level risk-of-bias assessment\.
- Some emerging medication findings were available only as abstracts or incomplete trial reports\.
- The authors report that treatment trials did not include patients with concurrent rheumatic diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus, limiting generalizability\.
- Fibromyalgia heterogeneity and limited evaluation of combined medication and nonpharmacological treatment complicate comparisons and individualized treatment conclusions\.
- Evidence labels are not entirely consistent: massage is described as weak evidence in the treatment box but moderate evidence in the conclusions\.

## Why this is relevant

- Highlights research gaps in long-term outcomes, standardized measurement, subgroup responses, and combined interventions\.
