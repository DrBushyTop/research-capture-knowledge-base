# Relationship between food waste, diet quality, and environmental sustainability

## Source facts

Zach Conrad, Meredith T\. Niles, Deborah A\. Neher, Eric D\. Roy, Nicole E\. Tichenor, Lisa Jahns

DOI: 10\.1371/journal\.pone\.0195405

<https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0195405>

## Machine interpretation

This 2018 research article combines US dietary survey data, national food-loss estimates, and biophysical modeling to examine consumer-level food waste and its relationship with diet quality and agricultural resource use\. Estimated daily waste was 422 g per person, with approximately 30 million acres of cropland used annually to produce uneaten food\. Higher Healthy Eating Index-2015 scores were associated with more estimated food waste and greater embodied irrigation-water and pesticide use, but less embodied cropland use\. These are observational, model-dependent associations rather than direct measurements of individual waste or evidence that improving diet quality causes increased waste\.

## Key findings

- Estimated daily consumer food waste was 421\.5 g per person \(95% CI: 409\.1–433\.9\), approximately 26% of food available by weight\.
- Fruits, vegetables, and their mixed dishes accounted for 38\.9% of estimated waste by weight, followed by dairy \(17\.1%\), meat and mixed meat dishes \(13\.5%\), and grains and mixed grain dishes \(12\.1%\)\.
- Estimated wasted energy was approximately 817 kcal per person daily, with a reported interval of 795–840 kcal, or about 29% of available daily energy\.
- Producing wasted food required an estimated 30\.02 million acres of cropland annually \(95% CI: 29\.29–30\.76 million\), equivalent to 7\.7% of US harvested cropland\.
- Estimated food waste increased from 295 g/day in the lowest diet-quality quintile to 535 g/day in the highest quintile; the trend was statistically significant \(P \< 0\.001\)\.
- Higher diet quality was associated with less embodied cropland use \(P = 0\.029\), but greater embodied irrigation-water and pesticide use \(both P \< 0\.001\)\. The authors reported no corresponding association with fertilizer waste\.
- Annual resources embodied in wasted food included approximately 4\.2 trillion gallons of irrigation water, 780 million pounds of pesticides, and 5\.6 billion pounds of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potash fertilizers combined\.

## Limitations

- Food waste was inferred from reported consumption and national food-specific loss proportions, not directly measured for participants\. Actual waste rates may vary across individuals and diet-quality groups in ways the analysis cannot capture\.
- The cross-sectional design and modeled associations do not establish causality\. Applying food-specific waste proportions to dietary composition also makes estimated waste partly dependent on the foods used to characterize diet quality\.
- Waste estimates included cooking losses, such as fats and oils remaining in a pan; these may not fit all definitions of avoidable food waste\.
- Dietary intake relied on one self-reported 24-hour recall per participant\. Recall and social-desirability errors could affect both diet-quality scores and waste estimates; the authors note possible overestimation from over-reporting fruits and vegetables\.
- The model assumed a closed US food system with all food domestically produced, excluding the actual geography and production conditions of imported foods\. The authors suggest this could underestimate cropland requirements\.
- Only 92% of Food Commodity Intake Database foods were matched to food-loss data; unmatched foods were excluded\.
- Datasets covered different years, and chemical-use estimates for hay and pasture relied on communications with agricultural extension agents because survey data were unavailable\.
- Reported uncertainty reflects variation in dietary intake through modeling and does not necessarily encompass all uncertainty in loss rates, recipe matching, or agricultural assumptions\.
- Resource-use estimates are not direct measurements of environmental damage\. The analysis did not comprehensively assess greenhouse gas emissions, biodiversity, soil erosion, or regional differences in environmental impact\.
- Proposed consumer education and waste-reduction approaches were not tested for effectiveness in this study\.

## Why this is relevant

- Useful for research on tradeoffs between nutritional quality and environmental sustainability rather than treating sustainability as a single outcome\.
- Provides a method for integrating dietary recalls, ingredient-level recipes, food-loss proportions, and agricultural resource modeling\.
- Highlights the importance of distinguishing waste by weight, calories, nutrients, land use, and agricultural inputs\.
- Offers historical US baseline estimates, but does not establish current waste levels or the effectiveness of particular waste-reduction interventions\.
